\title{English--Italian Neural Machine Translation on Europarl}

\author{Vasileios Lalos}
\affiliation{\institution{}\country{}}
\author{Riccardo Tellarini}
\affiliation{\institution{}\country{}}

\begin{abstract}
This work investigates recurrent neural machine translation on the Europarl v7 corpus. We compare RNN and LSTM encoder--decoders, embedding initialisations, translation directions, and word- and character-level representations using BLEU and METEOR as reference metrics. The Italian--English word model outperforms the English--Italian model, achieving 9.60 BLEU and 33.94 METEOR. Character-level modelling provides no improvement because of the longer sequences. Bahdanau attention increases English--Italian BLEU from 7.32 to 18.41, particularly benefiting longer sentences. Finally, Italian--Swedish pivot translation through English performs similarly to direct translation, although oracle analysis reveals substantial error propagation between its two stages.
\end{abstract}


\maketitle

\section{Introduction}

Recurrent encoder--decoder models provide a simple approach to neural machine translation, but their performance is affected by architecture, sentence representation, translation direction and source length.

This project investigates these factors on the Europarl v7 corpus. After data exploration and preprocessing, we compare recurrent architectures, hyperparameters, embeddings, translation directions, and word- and character-level models. We then evaluate Bahdanau attention and Italian--Swedish pivot translation through English. All systems are assessed on held-out data using BLEU and METEOR, focusing on the causes of the observed performance differences.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec}

Preprocessing is applied at pair level to preserve alignment. We lowercase both languages to reduce case-related vocabulary sparsity and normalise whitespace to stabilise tokenisation. A pair is removed if either normalised side is empty or begins with \texttt{<}. This removes 802 of 190,912 pairs (0.42\%), all because of empty sides; no XML-like lines were found in the sample.

We retain punctuation, numbers, stopwords and inflected surface forms, applying neither stemming nor lemmatisation, because these elements may carry lexical and grammatical information required for translation.

\section{Neural Machine Translation}
\label{sec:nmt}

After punctuation-aware tokenisation, pairs with more than 40
whitespace-delimited words on either side are removed for computational
tractability. This leaves 157,519 pairs, split with seed 42 into 110,265
training (70\%), 15,751 validation (10\%) and 31,503 held-out test (20\%)
pairs. English and Italian vocabularies contain the 20,000 most frequent
training tokens, validation and test data therefore cannot influence them.

As metrics for all the following experiments, we report corpus BLEU and mean sentence-level METEOR on a 0--100 scale. BLEU
uses NLTK's method-1 smoothing. METEOR uses language-specific Snowball stemming
but no synonym matching, because equivalent lexical resources were not
available for both languages. Validation loss selects models, the test set is
evaluated only once after all choices have been fixed.

\subsection{Architecture and training}

The baseline is a one-layer, unidirectional encoder--decoder with vanilla
\texttt{tanh} RNN units. The encoder consumes dynamically padded packed
sequences and represents the source by its final hidden state. The decoder is
initialised with this state and, to strengthen source conditioning without
introducing attention, concatenates the same fixed context vector to every
target-token embedding. This simple recurrent baseline directly satisfies the
task requirement and provides an interpretable reference point. We then replace
the RNN units with LSTMs: their gated memory is better suited to dependencies
across the retained 40-word sentences and alleviates vanishing gradients, while
the single-layer fixed-context design remains unchanged for a controlled
comparison.

Training uses full teacher forcing, cross-entropy loss with padding ignored,
Adam, batches of 128 and gradient clipping at norm 1.0. At inference, greedy
decoding starts from \texttt{<sos>} and stops at \texttt{<eos>} or after 80
tokens. Every run saves the checkpoint with minimum validation loss and uses
early stopping with patience three.

\subsection{Hyperparameter selection}

We tune manually, changing recurrent capacity, embedding size and learning rate
while keeping the data split, seed and training order fixed. All tuning runs use
the complete training and validation sets for at most 15 epochs. The primary
criterion is validation loss; BLEU and METEOR are diagnostic
(Table~\ref{tab:tuning}). Among vanilla RNNs, 64-dimensional embeddings, hidden
size 128 and learning rate 0.001 are best. Replacing this full-trained RNN with
an otherwise equivalent LSTM raises validation BLEU from 1.89 to 4.83 and
METEOR from 13.19 to 22.26. LSTM-specific tuning then selects
128-dimensional embeddings, hidden size 512 and learning rate 0.001.

\subsection{Embeddings}

The selected LSTM is compared with the same model initialised by separate
English and Italian Word2Vec models trained only on the training split. We use
CBOW, 128 dimensions, window five, minimum count two and 20 Word2Vec epochs;
the transferred vectors cover 97.9\% of the English vocabulary and 100\% of the
Italian vocabulary and remain trainable. Relative to embeddings learned from
scratch, Word2Vec reduces validation loss from 3.5314 to 3.2506 and increases
BLEU/METEOR from 6.80/26.68 to 7.20/28.25. It is therefore used by both final
word-level systems.

\subsection{Translation direction}
\label{sec:direction}

For Italian--English we swap source and target while retaining the same split,
vocabularies, architecture, Word2Vec initialisation, hyperparameters and
decoding procedure. It reaches 9.60 BLEU and 33.94 METEOR on the test set,
against 7.26 and 28.12 for English--Italian (Table~\ref{tab:finalnmt}). The
improvement may be explained by the fact that English has fewer inflected word
forms and a lower OOV rate, making it easier for a capped word vocabulary to
generate.

\subsection{Character-level model}
\label{sec:char}

The character model translates English to Italian with the same one-layer
fixed-context LSTM, 128-dimensional learned embeddings and hidden size 512. Its
training-only vocabularies contain 140 English and 136 Italian characters, so
there is effectively no word-level OOV problem. However, targets reach 343
characters and must be compressed into the same single context vector. The
model scores only 3.86 BLEU and 19.83 METEOR, below the
word model despite having fewer parameters (3.78\,M vs.\ 19.06\,M). It also
requires considerably more time to train, suggesting
that longer recurrent paths outweigh the vocabulary advantage. All timings in
Table~\ref{tab:finalnmt} were measured on a Tesla T4.

\subsection{Effect of sentence length and error analysis}
\label{sec:length}

Translation quality falls sharply as the source grows
(Table~\ref{tab:lengthnmt}). For the final English--Italian word model, BLEU
drops from 36.76 on 1--5-token inputs to 8.09 on 16--20-token inputs; METEOR
drops from 57.96 to 27.70. Italian--English and character-level translation show
the same trend. This diagnostic covers tokenised source lengths

Qualitative inspection explains the low corpus scores. Short, formulaic
Europarl expressions are often correct, but longer outputs replace source
details with generic parliamentary language, confuse proper names and numbers,
or repeat phrases until the decoding limit. Character outputs occasionally
recover an exact inflected form missed by the word model, but also create
plausible-looking non-words and repetitions. The consistent length degradation
supports the fixed-vector bottleneck diagnosis and motivates the attention
mechanism evaluated next.

\section{Attention}
\label{sec:attn}

We augment the fixed-context encoder--decoder of Section~\ref{sec:nmt} with
\textbf{Bahdanau (additive) attention}. At each decoding step the decoder scores
every encoder state against its current hidden state, normalises the scores with
a softmax (padded positions masked to $-\infty$), and consumes the resulting
weighted sum of encoder states, so the source is no longer squeezed through a
single vector. To measure attention as an improvement over Task~3, both the
no-attention baseline and the attention model use the \emph{identical} final
Task~3 pipeline (40-token cap, 20{,}000-type vocabulary, 128-dimensional
Word2Vec embeddings, hidden size 512, same split and seed); only the attention
mechanism differs.

On the held-out test set attention raises BLEU from 7.32 to 18.41 and METEOR
from 28.01 to 43.69 (Table~\ref{tab:attention}). The no-attention baseline
reproduces the Section~\ref{sec:nmt} English--Italian result (7.32 vs.\ 7.26),
confirming that the two are on the same footing and that the large gain is due to
attention alone. The cost is training time: because the context now depends on
the decoder state, the target is decoded step by step, tripling the training time
(166 vs.\ 53 minutes on a Tesla T4).

\subsection{Where the gain comes from}

If the fixed context vector is the bottleneck, attention should help longer
sentences most. Figure~\ref{fig:bleulen} confirms this: the gain grows with
source length, from $+7.0$ BLEU on 1--10-token inputs to $+11.6$ on 21--30 and
$+11.5$ on 31--40. Without attention, BLEU collapses from 18.2 to 5.6 as
sentences lengthen, whereas with attention it stays near 17--25 across all
lengths. Attention specifically repairs the long-sentence degradation observed in
Section~\ref{sec:length}.

\section{Pivot Translation}
\label{sec:pivot}

Europarl provides Italian--English and Swedish--English data but no
Italian--Swedish corpus. We therefore translate Italian to Swedish through
English as a \emph{pivot}: Italian--English (leg~1) followed by
English--Swedish (leg~2). Because the two corpora are aligned independently,
line $n$ of one is not the translation of line $n$ of the other, so we build the
missing evaluation corpus by joining them on their shared English side,
yielding 131,225 Italian--English--Swedish triples with all sides within the
40-token cap. These are split 70/10/20 with seed~42 into 91,858 training,
13,122 validation and 26,245 test triples. All systems use the Task~4 attention
architecture at the same configuration as Tasks~3 and~4 (embedding 128, hidden
512, Word2Vec-initialised, 20 epochs).

We compare four systems on the held-out test triples (Table~\ref{tab:pivot}):
each leg on its own, the chained pivot, and a direct Italian--Swedish model
trained on the same triples as an upper-bound comparator. The pivot reaches
12.04 BLEU, essentially matching the direct model (12.13, a gap of only
$0.09$)---so pivoting through English costs almost nothing relative to training
directly on the same data.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{Pivot translation on the held-out test triples. The oracle feeds leg~2
the \emph{reference} English; the pivot feeds it leg~1's own output.}
\label{tab:pivot}
\scriptsize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{System} & \textbf{BLEU} & \textbf{METEOR} \\
\midrule
IT$\rightarrow$EN (leg~1)                & 21.61 & 49.00 \\
EN$\rightarrow$SV (leg~2, oracle input)  & 19.19 & 48.18 \\
IT$\rightarrow$EN$\rightarrow$SV (pivot) & 12.04 & 36.30 \\
IT$\rightarrow$SV (direct)               & \textbf{12.13} & \textbf{38.06} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The oracle row isolates error propagation. Giving leg~2 the \emph{reference}
English yields 19.19 BLEU; feeding it leg~1's own output drops this to 12.04, so
$7.15$ BLEU---about a third of the achievable quality---is lost purely to
leg~1's mistakes, which the second leg cannot recover. The pivot retains only
$62.7\%$ of the oracle's performance. Error propagation, not the pivoting idea
itself, is therefore the real cost: the chain is close to a direct model
because both are limited by the same modest data, but a stronger first leg would
lift the whole pipeline.

\section{Conclusion}

Across all experiments, the single most important factor was the fixed-context
bottleneck. On the base encoder--decoder, translation quality was modest and
degraded sharply with sentence length, and replacing the vanilla RNN with an
LSTM and initialising it with Word2Vec embeddings gave only incremental gains.
Adding Bahdanau attention was decisive: English--Italian BLEU rose from 7.32 to
18.41, with the largest improvements on the long sentences that the fixed
context handled worst. Translation direction and representation mattered too ---
into-English translation was consistently easier than into-Italian, reflecting
Italian's richer morphology, while character-level modelling did not help
because of the much longer sequences. Finally, Italian--Swedish pivot
translation through English performed close to a direct model on BLEU, but the
oracle analysis showed that a substantial part of the pivot's errors is inherited
from the first leg, confirming that error propagation is the main cost of
pivoting. The clearest direction for further work is therefore to carry attention
into the pivot legs and to reduce first-leg errors, for example with beam search
or a shared multilingual vocabulary.
